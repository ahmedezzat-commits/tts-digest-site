Research paperPreprint

21Codec moves speaker identity into the streaming decoder

Lindevelt and colleagues use adversarial speaker suppression and formant-shift augmentation to reduce identity recoverable from codec tokens, supplying a speaker embedding to the decoder instead.

Why it matters A concrete codec experiment for reducing voice drift across streamed chunks. Decoder timings exclude text-to-token generation and network/player delay; no Arabic-specific result establishes transfer.

Evidence At 24 kHz and 0.8 kbps, batch-one ONNX decoding of 160 ms chunks reports RTF 0.129 on i9-13900K and 0.035 on RTX 4090. A 30-speaker English chunk sweep gives similarity 0.875 at 160 ms versus 0.881 non-causal. Five-language stability tests and listener re-voicing tests support identity control; cross-speaker re-voicing raises English WER from 0.099 to 0.140 versus same-speaker conditioning.

Research paperPreprint

Layer-wise prompting exposes a voice-versus-style conditioning trade-off

Lindevelt and colleagues compare concatenated, equal, style-early and voice-early prompt conditioning through fixed layer gates in a six-block codec-token TTS decoder.

Why it matters Conditioning depth is an experimental control when style prompts disturb agent voice identity. Both voice preservation and style expressiveness benefit from early layers, so the result is a trade-off rather than one universally best order.

Evidence English LibriTTS-R/LibriTTS-P: 515.1 hours, 2,295 speakers. Voice-early minimizes drift across ECAPA-TDNN, TitaNet and WavLM; style-early favors style spread. Mixed-effects comparisons and cluster-robust checks support conditioning effects, but proxy metrics do not establish Arabic transfer or perceptual superiority.
